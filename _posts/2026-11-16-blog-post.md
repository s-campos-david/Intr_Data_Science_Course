I started this project with a question: can we identify hospitals with poor maternal outcomes without looking at their complication score?

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) publishes maternal health measures and classifies scored hospitals as Top, Middle or Bottom Performers. I wanted to understand what else in the file might help explain those labels. What surprised me was how much the picture changed from one state to another, and how little the safety attestation seemed to add.

## The first thing that stood out was the map

The file contains 4,655 hospitals, but only 2,360 have a performance label. About one in four of those is a Bottom Performer.

Among states with at least 25 scored hospitals, that share ranges from about 8% in Kansas to 51% in New York. I expected to spend most of my time looking for differences between individual hospitals. Instead, the state comparison became one of the most interesting parts of the project.

But the map does not explain itself. Differences in patient populations, access to care, hospital services or reporting could contribute. This comparison cannot tell me whether a state provides worse care, records complications more thoroughly, or faces different circumstances.

For me, the next question is not "which state is bad?" It is "what is behind this gap?" State-level care and reporting practices seem worth investigating before treating the labels as straightforward hospital rankings.

## More cesareans, a higher share of Bottom Performers

I divided hospitals into five groups by their cesarean rate. The share of Bottom Performers rises across those groups, from 21.5% in the lowest group to 32.5% in the highest.

That is a pattern worth following up, not proof that cesareans caused the complications. The cesarean measure concerns first births in a defined lower-risk population, while the complication measure covers a different delivery population. A hospital-level comparison cannot tell us what caused an individual patient's outcome or which delivery was medically necessary.

## The safety attestation did not separate the groups

For SM_7, the maternal safety attestation, the result was almost flat: 25.1% of hospitals reporting "Yes" were Bottom Performers, compared with 24.7% among other responses.

These are shares of hospitals with a label, not complication rates among patients. Also, "other responses" does not necessarily mean a hospital said "No". I would not conclude that safety programs do nothing. What I can say is that this comparison gave me little reason to use the attestation alone as a guide to the performance label.

## The most accurate model found nobody

A baseline that predicts no hospital is a Bottom Performer gets 74.9% accuracy. It also finds exactly zero Bottom Performers.

Logistic regression gets a lower accuracy, 60.3%, but identifies about half of them. The trade-off is substantial: only about one in three flags is correct. Its ROC AUC is 0.623, suggesting some ability to separate the groups, but far from a reliable hospital assessment.

This was the clearest machine-learning lesson for me. Accuracy rewards the baseline for ignoring the group I wanted to find. Whether a model helps depends on what the mistakes cost, not just how many predictions it gets right.

## What I would do next

The model uses cesarean rate, delivery volume, state group and a rough academic-affiliation flag taken from the hospital name. I excluded the complication rate because its ranges already separate the three performance labels: using it would give away the answer.

The labels themselves also hide differences. Bottom Performer includes complication scores from 420 to 4,531. Predicting that category does not tell us where a hospital falls within it, and those numbers should not be read as a simple ratio of care quality.

Before trying more complicated models, I would look for information about staffing, patient characteristics and the communities hospitals serve. I have not proved that the available data sets a ceiling on prediction; I just have good reasons to explore what is missing.

There are validation limits too. I explored the full labelled sample before the split and used it to group states. The model was tested on one split, and its scores are not calibrated risks. Hospitals without a CMS label were not part of that evaluation, so I cannot assume the same performance for them.

I came away with a weak model and better questions. Why do the state shares differ so much? What does the safety attestation capture? Which missing information would help us understand outcomes more fairly? I would rather pursue those questions than use these predictions to judge a hospital.
